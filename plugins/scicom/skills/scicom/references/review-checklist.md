# Review checklist

Run this before delivering any draft, and when reviewing someone else's. Fix failures rather than just listing them. When reporting a review, start with the most important issues: accuracy, hype, then message.

Adapted from the CDC Clear Communication Index, the QUEST quality indicators and checklists, the Science Media Centre guidelines for reporting science, and the AMS/SMC press-release labelling system.

## 1. Message
- [ ] One main message, stated in the first two or three sentences (or the first screen, slide or seconds).
- [ ] The reader knows why it matters to them.
- [ ] A clear next step or call to action, where relevant: read, attend, apply, contact, consider. Say how easy it is (time, place, what is needed). For invitations: one specific action, from a named person.

## 2. Audience and readability
- [ ] Written for the stated audience, not for peers.
- [ ] Sentences average 20 words or fewer; active voice; few nominalisations.
- [ ] Every technical term is necessary and explained once, with an example. For lay readers, at most one unfamiliar word in 50, counting repeats (Rakedzon et al. 2017), and one or two acronyms.
- [ ] No word with a different public meaning is left unexplained (theory, uncertainty, error, significant).
- [ ] Headings and headline say what happens; the text can be scanned.

## 3. Accuracy
- [ ] Every claim can be traced to the source material or to the user. Nothing invented.
- [ ] Names, titles, affiliations, journal, date and DOI are correct, or marked `[CHECK]`.
- [ ] Numbers match the source, with units, and are rounded sensibly.
- [ ] The study type and status are stated (peer-reviewed or preprint; theory, simulation, experiment, observation, trial).
- [ ] Model systems are named (cells, mice, lab prototype, simulation).
- [ ] Causal language is used only for causal evidence.
- [ ] No extrapolation beyond what the results show; timelines only if the researchers give and justify them.

## 4. Claims and hype
- [ ] Scanned for hype words, including inside quotes, using the list in `press-and-media.md`. Each one is justified or removed.
- [ ] Headline and lede are no stronger than the paper's own conclusions.
- [ ] Hope is kept apart from findings.

## 5. Uncertainty and limitations
- [ ] The one to three limitations and conditions that change the conclusion are stated in plain words.
- [ ] Uncertainty shown concretely (ranges, confidence, what remains unknown) rather than through vague hedging.
- [ ] If there is debate or contrary evidence, it is acknowledged, with the reason experts disagree.

## 6. Numbers and risk
- [ ] Few numbers, each with context.
- [ ] Absolute next to relative; one format and one denominator for everything compared; benefits and harms in the same format.

## 7. Credit and fairness
- [ ] Co-authors, early-career researchers, technical staff, partners and funders are credited, with any required funding acknowledgement (communicators).
- [ ] Partner institutions are named correctly and consulted where needed.
- [ ] Where useful, an independent perspective is included or suggested.

## 8. Inclusion and accessibility
- [ ] Inclusive language; diverse representation in images and quotes where possible.
- [ ] Ethnicity, nationality, religion or sexual orientation mentioned only when needed to understand the story.
- [ ] Alt text for every image, captions for video, readable colour contrast, link text that makes sense out of context.

## 9. Legal and ethical
- [ ] Embargo respected, and the embargo line is correct.
- [ ] Consent for people in photos or video (parents for minors); image credits and licences.
- [ ] No disclosure that could harm a patent application; sensitive or dual-use details checked.
- [ ] Confidential or personal data removed.
- [ ] Competing interests declared; anyone criticised by name had a chance to respond.
- [ ] AI use disclosed if required.
- [ ] Quotes approved by the people quoted; drafted quotes clearly marked as drafts (communicators; journalists: see the block below).

## 10. Format fit
- [ ] Length and structure match the format (see the format file).
- [ ] Platform conventions followed (characters, hooks, hashtags, links last).
- [ ] Two or three headline or hook options offered where useful.

## 11. Dialogue
- [ ] It acknowledges what the audience may already think or feel about the topic, and when a fact is hard to believe.
- [ ] No labels such as "denier" or "anti-science", and no war frames.
- [ ] It invites questions or a response, where the format allows.
- [ ] It neither overstates nor understates: a genuinely significant result is called significant.

## Journalist role (instead of approvals and credits)
- [ ] "Says who?" answered for every claim ("experts say": how many? "raises hopes": whose?); at least one independent voice.
- [ ] The release checked against the paper.
- [ ] Comment sought from anyone criticised; no copy approval given or promised.
- [ ] Funding and conflicts reported as context.
- [ ] Quotes checked against recordings or notes.
- [ ] Headline, captions and social text don't overclaim together; hype words and emotional cue words ("shocking", "alarming") only in attributed outside quotes, never in the headline.

## Final tests

- Would the researcher sign off on every sentence? (Communicators; journalists: would every sentence survive the source's fact check, and would the institution behind the release like this story more than it should?)
- Would a sceptical science journalist trust it? How big is the gain, for whom, at what cost? Funding and interests match the paper's declarations; the journal is not predatory.
- Would a 15-year-old get the gist?
- Would it still be accurate if someone read only the headline and first sentence?
