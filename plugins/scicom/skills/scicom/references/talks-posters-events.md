# Talks, pitches, posters and events

## Talks (Bourne 2007; Naegle 2021)

- **Start from the audience:** what do they know, what do they need, and how much time is there?
- **One main message** plus the three points a listener should recall a week later (Bourne). Open and close with it.
- **Story arc:** open with the result, a puzzle or an early failure, not the paper's order. Then why it matters, what we did, and what it means.
- **Slides in assertion–evidence style:** a full-sentence headline stating the point, plus a visual that supports it.
  - Few words, one idea per slide, readable from the back of the room.
  - No dense bullet lists.
  - Build complex diagrams over several slides; explain the axes before the result; put backup slides after the conclusion; credit contributors on the slide that shows their work.
  - Accessible: sans-serif, large type, bold for emphasis (no italics or all caps), high contrast, colour-blind-safe palettes, no decorative animation.
- **Timing:** start from roughly one slide per minute, and roughly 130–150 spoken words a minute (practice): a 3MT script is about 350–400 words. Rehearse aloud with a timer, then for friendly colleagues, and leave time for questions. Bring a PDF backup, and a still for every video.
- **Engage:** ask a question, show an object, give a demonstration, tell a story.
- **Questions and answers:** repeat the question, answer briefly, and admit what you don't know. Then bridge back to the message. If a questioner turns disruptive, offer to continue afterwards. If someone is angry or grieving, listen and acknowledge before explaining.
- **Online:**
  - camera at eye level, good light and sound, and look into the lens;
  - shorter segments;
  - test screen sharing, and use the chat or polls to interact.

## Short pitches and the Three Minute Thesis (3MT)

- **3MT rules:** over 3:00 means disqualification. One static slide, on screen from the start. No sound, video, props, poems or songs. Timing starts at the first word or movement. Plain language for non-specialists. Check the organiser's current rules.
- **Check the script** against the judging criteria: context and significance, every term explained, results and impact, balanced time, not trivialised, leaves listeners wanting more.
- **Structure** (practice):
  1. a hook: a story or a question;
  2. the problem;
  3. your approach;
  4. what you found, or hope to find;
  5. why it matters;
  6. a closing line that echoes the hook.
- **Elevator pitch** (30–60 seconds): who you are, the problem, what you do, why it matters, and an invitation to talk further.
- **Rehearse** until it sounds like talking, not reciting.

## Posters (Erren & Bourne 2007; The Online Scientist)

- **Title** = the finding, in plain language.
- **First decide** what a passer-by should do: discuss, try the method, collaborate.
- **Main message:** one large statement in the centre, readable from three metres away, supported by a few figures. About 600 words at most in total (The Online Scientist). Conclusions next to the title.
- **Layout:** a clear reading order (columns or one central visual), white space, consistent colours.
- **QR code** to the paper, the slides or a short video.
- **Prepare** a one-minute pitch and a few questions for visitors; let them read before you pitch. Add your photo and contact details. The poster is there to start conversations.

## Public events

Formats: science cafés, festivals, open days, science slams, public lectures, museum collaborations, citizen-science events.

- **Interaction over lecturing:** hands-on demonstrations, objects, questions, polls.
- **Connect** to people's interests and daily lives, and invite questions from the start.
- **Know who comes:** café and lecture audiences already like science. Set goals to match, and partner with schools or community groups to reach beyond them.
- **To hear views,** use small-group formats (World Café, citizen panels), not open-mic Q&A. Agree up front what participants' input will influence, and report back afterwards. Don't claim representativeness, and never use dialogue to win acceptance for a decision already taken.
- **Role models:** visible and diverse. Let PhD candidates and technicians present too.
- **Practicalities:**
  - accessibility: venue, captions, quiet spaces;
  - language, timing and the calendar (holidays, religious days);
  - no all-male panels;
  - safety;
  - notices about photography and consent.
- **Evaluate simply:** a short QR survey, including "Did the researchers seem open to your views?", a show of hands, observation. It measures experience, not impact.

## Schools and young audiences

- **Fit the curriculum and the teacher's goals.** Agree the age group and timing in advance.
- **Language for the age:** concrete examples and everyday analogies; nothing abstract without a picture. Under about 11, short phrases, repetition and pictures, one step at a time; from about 11, stress the meaning.
- **Hands-on activities** with real objects. Show what scientists actually do and who they are, including careers and science in everyday life.
- **Pacing:** short segments of 10–15 minutes with activities in between. Leave materials for the teacher.
- **Safeguarding:** follow the school's rules, and take no photos of pupils without consent.
