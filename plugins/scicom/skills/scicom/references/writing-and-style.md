# Writing and style

## Process

1. **Write the main message in one sentence.** If you can't, you are not ready to write yet.
2. **Outline:** what does the reader need first, second, third?
3. **Draft quickly;** polish later.
4. **Cut.** Aim to remove a fifth; every paragraph must earn its place.
5. **Read aloud.** Rewrite wherever you stumble. Then re-read from the top as a reader who knows only what the text has said so far, and look for words that can be read the wrong way. Don't let a quote carry an explanation you couldn't write yourself.
6. **Misreading test:** what is the most wrong story a reporter on deadline could write from the headline and lede alone? Cut images and allusions that fail it; where a loaded reading is likely ("teleportation"), add one "this does not mean…" line.

## Structures

- **Inverted pyramid** (news): the most important information first. Each paragraph matters less than the one before, so readers can stop anywhere.
- **Feature:** a scene or person → the nut graf (what this is about, the wider pattern, and why now) → a body alternating story and explanation → a kicker that echoes the opening or looks ahead.
- **Explainer:** question → short answer → how we know → what is uncertain → what's next.
- **And, but, therefore:** "[context] and [context], but [problem], therefore [what we did or found]". Use it to test any pitch, abstract or opening.
- **Frames:** social progress; economic development and competitiveness; morality and ethics; scientific uncertainty; Pandora's box; public accountability and governance; middle way; conflict and strategy (Nisbet 2009). Pick one that fits what the audience already thinks. Opponents can turn any frame round, so pair progress or competitiveness with the technology's real readiness.
- **"So what?" test:** ask it after each paragraph. If there is no answer for the reader, cut the paragraph or connect it.

## Audience levels and layered writing

| Level | Reader | Write |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | General public: school-level science | concrete, warm, one idea at a time; wonder is welcome |
| 2 | Tech-literate: comfortable with logic, data, probability | mechanism over metaphor, still plain |
| 3 | Scientist from another field | what is new, what is hard, how it connects to their field |
| 4 | Specialist in the field | significance, context, open questions; little hand-holding |
| S | Decision-makers: policy, industry, funders | stakes, options, timelines, decisions |

- **Mixed channels** (website, LinkedIn, newsletters) reach several levels at once, and knowledge of a field is often polarised: many readers know almost nothing, a few know a lot. Write the headline and first two paragraphs for Level 1, then go deeper for readers who continue.
- **Adjust the depth, never the respect.**

## Six story types

Pick the one that matches what the work actually contributes. Don't force drama onto a solid incremental result.

- **The quest:** people have tried to do X for years; this found a way.
- **The surprise:** everyone assumed X; this shows otherwise.
- **The bridge:** it connects two ideas that seemed unrelated.
- **The threshold:** it pushes past a limit that seemed firm.
- **The tool:** it gives researchers a new way to do X, which matters because…
- **The milestone:** one step closer to X, and here is where things stand. Useful for decision-makers.

**Basic research:** don't lean on "curiosity-driven research leads to unforeseen breakthroughs". Lead with the question, the people, their enjoyment of the work, and what it makes possible at its current stage.

## Sentences

- **Length:** average 20 words or fewer; vary the length; one idea per sentence.
- **Active voice by default:** "The team measured…", not "Measurements were performed…". The passive is fine when the actor doesn't matter.
- **Verbs, not nouns:** "analyse", not "carry out an analysis of".
- **Familiar before new:** put familiar information first and new information at the end, where stress falls. Keep subject and verb close together.
- **Break noun stacks:** "error-correction threshold estimation method" becomes "a method to estimate how many errors the system can correct".
- **Positive form:** "forgot", not "did not remember".
- **Parallel structure** in lists.
- **Cut needless words:**
  - "in order to" → "to";
  - "a number of" → "several";
  - "at this point in time" → "now";
  - "it is important to note that" → delete.

## Words

- **Everyday words:** "use", not "utilise"; "help", not "facilitate"; "show", not "demonstrate".
- **One term for one thing:** don't vary synonyms ("qubit", "quantum bit", "the bit"). Readers assume a new word means a new thing.
- **Show, don't announce:** cut "interestingly", "remarkably", "very", "we are delighted to announce"; give the detail instead.
- **Jargon:** cut it, replace it with an everyday word, or keep it and explain it once with an example. Test drafts on a non-expert: ask them to say in their own words what it means ("Is it clear?" gets a misleading yes). A jargon checker such as the De-Jargonizer helps, but misses everyday words used technically ("fatigue" in materials science). Headline test: would readers understand the word in a headline ("DNA" yes, "RNA" no)? Plain words make researchers look more competent, not less. Readability scores count only word and sentence length: a rough flag, not a test.
- **Acronyms:** avoid them; a generic name ("the detector", "the consortium") often works. If one is needed, spell it out at first mention, in lower case unless it is a proper name: quantum key distribution (QKD). Use no more than one or two per text, and don't define PhD or DNA.
- **Loaded words:** don't adopt one side's euphemisms, use "war on science" or battle frames, or tie a topic to an unrelated contested issue.
- **Words the public reads differently** (based on Somerville & Hassol 2011, plus common cases):

| Scientists say | The public may hear | Try instead |
|---|---|---|
| theory | a hunch | well-tested explanation |
| uncertainty | ignorance | range, margin |
| error | mistake | margin of error, difference from the true value |
| bias | prejudice | systematic offset |
| significant | important | statistically significant / large enough to matter |
| positive feedback | praise | self-reinforcing loop |
| manipulation | illicit tampering | change, control |
| model | a small replica | computer simulation, mathematical description |
| sign | indication | plus or minus sign |
| values | ethics | numbers |
| anomaly | abnormal event | difference from the long-term average |
| enhance | improve | increase, intensify |
| scheme | a devious plot | systematic plan |
| chaos | total disorder | sensitive dependence on starting conditions |
| plasma | blood plasma | ionised gas |

## Metaphors and analogies

- **Precise first, then vivid,** never the other way round. A direct, concrete explanation often does more than a metaphor.
- **Use analogies mainly as guardrails** against misconceptions. Choose something the audience knows, run it past the researcher, check that it matches the right quantity (peak power or energy; per pulse or per second), and say where the comparison breaks down ("unlike real coins…"). If the breakdown is interesting, it can be the hook.
- **One analogy per idea;** don't mix them.
- **Don't use mystique instead of explanation:** "spooky", "weird", "magic", "mind-bending". In a representative Dutch experiment on quantum technology, calling it "enigmatic" had no measurable effect on engagement, while explaining the physics raised self-reported interest (Meinsma et al. 2026a).
- **Feeling less sure is fine.** A good explanation can leave readers feeling less sure while understanding more. Aim for understanding, not for readers feeling clever. In a Dutch experiment on quantum news (1,167 adults), explanations raised actual understanding but lowered how well readers felt they understood the article, and metaphorical explanations did no better than plain ones (Meinsma, Reijnierse & Cramer 2026b).
- **Explain what a thing does before giving its name:** readers take everyday-sounding terms ("superposition", "entanglement") in their everyday sense.
- **Metaphors carry values.** Check that yours do not reinforce stereotypes.
- **Writing about AI:** don't give systems human traits, and check developers' claims before repeating them.

## Headlines and titles

For a story with a lot to explain, the lede can be followed by the real order of events, told as a mystery being solved, instead of the inverted pyramid.

- Say what was found or what happens. Include a verb and be specific.
- News headlines: about 12 words at most. Web titles: about 60 characters, keywords first.
- No question headlines unless the subhead answers them, no puns that hide the meaning, no clickbait.
- The subhead adds the why or the context.
- Test: if a reader reads only the headline, is what they take away still accurate?
- Watch for ambiguity, especially in noun-heavy titles written by non-native speakers.

Also watch for:
- very long sentences with the verb at the end;
- "allows to" (write "allows us to"), "informations" and "researches" ("information", "studies"), "in case of" meaning "if";
- an over-formal tone;
- Title Case Headlines. British style uses sentence case.

## Conventions (default: British English, EU style)

Follow the house style first. Otherwise use the European Commission English Style Guide:
- **Spelling:** British, with -ise (organise, realise), but keep an organisation's own spelling (World Health Organization). Also analyse, sulfur, and program for software.
- **Headlines and headings:** sentence case, no full stop, no colon at the end.
- **Commas:** no serial comma before the final "and" or "or", unless needed to avoid ambiguity.
- **Dashes:** spaced en dashes ( – ), no em dashes.
- **Capitals:** when in doubt, lower case: policies and programmes (except brand names such as Horizon Europe), roles outside a full title, internet, web, seasons. No capital after a colon.
- **Numbers:**
  - one to nine in words, 10 and above in figures; when both occur together, use figures ("9 to 11");
  - always figures with units, decimals and percentages, and for all numbers on the web and social media;
  - don't start a sentence with a figure: spell it out or rephrase;
  - "three times faster" in running text; "3×" only in charts, tables and on-screen text;
  - thousands: 15 000 with a hard space in documents, or 15,000 in web and promotional text, as the house style says;
  - 2.5 million; a decimal point, not a comma.
- **Percentages and money:**
  - 25% (closed up), but "twenty per cent" when spelled out;
  - €2 400 and €2.4 million in releases and on the web; EUR 2 400 in legal or formal documents.
- **Dates and times:**
  - 28 October 2026 (no ordinal, no comma);
  - 24-hour clock, 09:30 and 14:00, in formal texts.
- **Quotation marks:** curly single quotes for quotations, double quotes for quotes within quotes. Journal titles in italics (*Nature Physics*); article titles in single quotes.
- **Names:** in news, full name and role at first mention, then surname alone; no Dr or Prof. in running text (only in quote attributions, if the house style wants it), unless the house style says otherwise.
- **Units:** SI, with a space between number and unit (5 kg, 20 °C).

## Web readability (content design)

- Front-load: key information in the first two lines and in the headings.
- A heading every three to five paragraphs, stating the point or the reader's question.
- Paragraphs of one to three sentences; lists for steps and options.
- Link text that says where it goes ("read the paper", not "click here").

## Inclusive language and representation

- Gender-neutral terms: chair, staff, they; human-made, not man-made.
- Don't name diseases or pathogens after places, peoples or groups.
- Respectful terms: older people, not "the elderly"; people with a condition, not "suffering from" it.
- No "layman"; no "simply" or "obviously".
- Person-first language where people prefer it.
- Don't assume a male, Western or able-bodied default.
- Show and quote diverse researchers, and credit early-career researchers and technical staff; keep a simple tally of whom you quote and put forward, and offer journalists more than one expert.
