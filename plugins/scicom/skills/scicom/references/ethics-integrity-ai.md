# Ethics, integrity, AI use and researcher safety

## Principles for institutional research communication

Based on the *Guidelines for Good Research Communication* (Wissenschaft im Dialog, 2025), the *Netherlands Code of Conduct for Research Integrity* (2018) and the *European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity* (ALLEA, 2023):

- **Honesty and accuracy:** present results as they are, with their limitations, without exaggeration.
- **Transparency:** name sources, funders, partners and relevant interests. Declare more than money: patent and spin-off stakes, board roles, earlier public positions. Interests don't disqualify you; hiding them does. Check declared interests against the institution's public register of ancillary activities (nevenwerkzaamheden). Be clear about what kind of text it is: institutional communication is not independent journalism.
- **Separate** facts, claims, interpretation and opinion (a researcher's "first" is a claim to attribute), and mark speculation as speculation.
- **Respect audiences:** be understandable and don't manipulate.
- **Independence:** communication goals must not shape results or how they are presented.
- **Correct errors** promptly and visibly.
- **Credit** co-authors, collaborators, technical staff and earlier work.
- **Expertise:** researchers speaking in public should stay within their field, and say whether they are giving a professional judgement, a personal view or the institution's position.

## When promotion and the public interest conflict

Competition for funds, students and attention pushes universities towards promotional, PR-style communication, which audiences trust less (Weingart & Joubert 2019; Marcinkowski & Kohring 2014; both opinion essays). For evidence that exaggeration often starts in releases, see Sumner et al. in `press-and-media.md`. When the two conflict, accuracy and the public interest win.

A practical test: would you be comfortable if an independent expert and a journalist compared your text with the paper, line by line?

## Consent, privacy and rights

- **People in photos and video:** get consent from identifiable people, in writing for published material. Minors need consent from parents or guardians. Tell people where the material will appear and for how long. Respect withdrawal of consent. GDPR applies in the EU.
- **Research participants:** never identify them without explicit consent.
- **Images and music:** check licences and credit creators. Use the institution's image bank or properly attributed openly licensed images.
- **Quotes:** approved by the person quoted. When editing a quote, never change its meaning.

## Embargoes, unpublished results, patents and security

- **Embargoes:** respect journal embargoes and conference rules. Don't publicise unpublished results without the researchers' agreement.
- **Patents:** any public disclosure before a patent application is filed can destroy patentability. That includes a talk, a post, a press release or a preprint. Check with the technology transfer office first.
- **Knowledge security:** for sensitive or dual-use research (some areas of quantum technology, AI, semiconductors, biotechnology), check with the research security officer before publishing technical details or partner information.
- **Industry agreements:** check what may be said, and when. Name industry partners plus one safeguard (who controls publication, whether data are open), and never claim more independence than that. Consortium partners may have approval rights, so allow lead time. Industry-funded research is trusted far less in the Netherlands, so let a university researcher speak to the findings.
- **Fronting a campaign:** fronting an outside organisation's release or campaign is advocacy: fine if declared. Check who funds it first (EU Transparency Register).
- **Animal research:** name the species and the ethical approval, mention the 3Rs (replace, reduce, refine), and follow the institution's openness policy.

## Using AI in communication work

Based on the EU *Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research* (fourth version, October 2026) and practice:

- **People stay responsible** for everything that is published. AI output is a draft.
- **Verify** every fact, number, name and reference against the source. Never write from an AI summary of a paper; work from the paper itself.
- **Never fabricate quotes** (practice), and never attribute AI-written words to a person without their approval. The Digital News Report 2026 records that publisher Mediahuis suspended a former NRC editor-in-chief over AI-fabricated quotes in his newsletter.
- **Protect material:** don't put confidential, unpublished, embargoed or personal data into AI tools that are not approved for it. If you use AI transcription or note-taking in interviews, tell the interviewee.
- **Be transparent:** disclose AI use that shapes content or judgement (drafting, summarising or interpreting sources); spelling, grammar and formatting need none. The European code counts hiding AI use in creating content as an unacceptable practice, so flag AI drafting of texts under a researcher's name. Follow the institution's, funder's or outlet's policy where it asks for more. By law, AI-generated text published without human review to inform the public on matters of public interest must be labelled (EU AI Act Art. 50(4)); see `automation.md`.
- **AI images** (practice): label them, and never use them to depict real experiments, data, people or events.
- **Watch for bias** in AI output, such as stereotyped images of scientists or Western defaults (practice). AI tools tend to tell users what they want to hear; don't let a push for punch move claims beyond the source.

## Researcher safety

Visibility brings risk. Researchers who appear in the media, especially on contested topics, report hostile reactions, harassment and threats. Women, ethnic minorities and researchers on politicised topics (climate, gender, migration, pandemics) are targeted more often; the most visible report the most attacks, and early-career and temporary staff have the least protection (Mede 2026; ALLEA 2023).

- **Prepare:** discuss the sensitivity of the topic beforehand; let the press office handle media requests; keep private details private; agree in advance how to respond to abuse. If the researcher was threatened before: ask whether they still want to go ahead; check that earlier threats were reported; name one person to monitor channels; keep home town and private details off air and online; agree visible support and a debrief afterwards.
- **Respond:** don't engage with abuse; document it with screenshots; report it to the institution; contact the police for threats. For legal threats over public statements, involve the legal office at once. In the Netherlands, report to WetenschapVeilig (wetenschapveilig.nl, 24/7); the employer then arranges security, legal and mental-health support. Report lower-level abuse too.
- **Support:** institutions should stand by their researchers visibly, publicly where appropriate.
