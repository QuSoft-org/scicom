# Uncertainty, risk, hype and misinformation

## Communicating uncertainty

**Be clear about five things** (after van der Bles et al. 2019):
- **What** is uncertain: a fact, a number or estimate, or the science itself (competing explanations).
- **How much:** a range or probability, wherever possible.
- **Why:** measurement limits, small samples, model assumptions, missing data, disagreement; and whether more research would narrow it (missing data, small samples) or not (natural variability, chance).
- **In what form:** numbers (ranges), words (used carefully), or visuals (ranges, intervals, fan charts).
- **How good** the underlying evidence is, stated separately from how big the effect is.

**Principles** (Uncertainty Handbook; Sense about Science; practice):
- Uncertainty is normal in science. Say so up front, without apologising.
- Lead with what is known and agreed, then say what is uncertain.
- Where there is a number, give the best estimate and the range where the number first appears, with a plain reading ("most likely 4 hours, between 3 and 5"), rather than vague words ("may", "possibly"). Never a bare "95% CI" for lay readers. For causal claims from correlational evidence, "may" is the right word (see the calibration table in `press-and-media.md`).
- Name the kind of uncertainty: not yet known; natural variability; model assumptions; disagreement among experts.
- When experts disagree, say why: different data, different methods, a question still open. Otherwise lay readers conclude the experts are incompetent. Disagree without questioning motives.
- Say what would change the conclusion, and what new data are coming. If the result is early or likely to be revised, say so now.
- Give the one to three caveats that change what readers should conclude, ideally in the researchers' own words (attributed, or a `[QUOTE]` placeholder for approval). A pile of qualifications hides the message.
- Uncertainty is not a reason for inaction when the risks are clear. Explain what is robust enough to act on. In policy advice, the threshold for acting is the decision-maker's (see `policy.md`).
- Where consensus exists, say so with a figure and a named source (preferably national or European bodies), and say how many lines of evidence agree. Never inflate it. With sceptical or polarised audiences, explain the evidence first rather than leading with "scientists agree".
- If you use probability words ("likely"), add the number first: "about 70% (likely)". People read these words very differently. In briefs and reports, give both how likely the outcome is and how confident the experts are (amount of evidence, level of agreement).
- If the probability can't be estimated, say so and why; don't write "unlikely" or "possible".
- **Models and scenarios:** name the one or two assumptions the result depends on most, and give the range.
- Match the verb to the evidence in both directions: "the evidence shows" for settled findings, not "scientists believe"; don't shrink a main cause to "plays a role".

**Three kinds of uncertainty readers recognise.** Say which one applies, and be concrete:
- **Process:** we are still finding out (more experiments needed, a new method, theory not yet tested). Frame it as an open frontier, not a failure. Frame a failed replication or a correction as a problem being worked on (what went wrong, how it was found, what is being done), never as a way to play down misconduct.
- **Context:** it may not hold outside these conditions. Name the conditions: "on a seven-qubit chip near absolute zero" says more than "further research is needed".
- **Impact:** we don't yet know what it means for society. Separate what the result makes possible in principle from what it delivers in practice.

**Evidence:** in experiments, a numeric range lowered trust in the number only slightly and hardly affected trust in the source; vague verbal uncertainty lowered it more. The evidence is still thin (van der Bles et al. 2019, 2020).

## Risk and numbers (FDA evidence-based guide; Science Media Centre guidelines; practice)

- **Absolute before relative:** "risk rises from 2 in 1 000 to 3 in 1 000", not just "50% higher risk". The same goes for performance claims: "errors halved" becomes "from 1 in 100 to 1 in 200 operations".
- **Same format and denominator** for everything compared: "3 in 100 versus 8 in 100", not "3% versus 1 in 12". For small risks, a larger denominator ("3 in 10 000"). For a key probability, give both sides ("works in 9 of 10 runs, fails in 1").
- Give the **baseline**, use one **time frame** for everything compared, and add up repeated exposure for the reader (per run versus per year).
- **Risks with benefits:** give both, in the same format, plus what happens if nothing is done.
- **Round sensibly:** "about 40%", not "39.7%".
- **Do the arithmetic:** give the difference, ratio or converted unit, not two numbers to compare.
- **No bare adjectives:** never "high", "low" or "large" without a number and a comparison. Next to the number, a plain gist is fine ("rare: about 2 in 100"); "well below the limit" only against a recognised threshold.
- **Percentages:** "percentage points" for differences between percentages; "15 times", not "1,400%"; the median when outliers skew the mean.
- **Comparisons:** use familiar ones, but don't trivialise.
- **Visuals:** icon arrays with the numbers on them (baseline and extra cases in two colours); bars to compare options; lines for change over time.
- **Hazard versus risk:** separate what could cause harm from how likely and how serious the harm is. Explain in one sentence why the risk is as large or small as it is, aimed at the likely misconception, and who monitors it.
- **Threat plus response:** pair every threat with a response that works and a step people can take. A threat alone gets played down. Say what remains fine: a limited warning is often heard as "avoid it altogether".
- **Perceived risk:** risks feel worse when involuntary, uncontrollable, unfair (some bear it, others benefit), catastrophic, unfamiliar or poorly understood, or managed by people the audience distrusts. Say which apply and how each is handled; don't dismiss the concern.
- **Scope:** say whom and what conditions the numbers apply to, and what each percentage is out of (per person, per run, per year). For "% of the limit", say which quantity (field strength, power density, dose) and which limit.
- **Rankings and indices:** report the band ("in the top 100"), not the exact rank; say who built the index and what goes into it. Don't make news of small rank changes: if asked anyway, write a short website item, not a release.
- **Translate statistics:** statistical significance is not importance; correlation is not causation; one study is not a consensus.
- **Small counts** (10 incidents versus 4): give the counts, not only the percentage change, and say that chance and changes in reporting can't be excluded.
- **Very large or very small numbers** (common in physics and computer science):
  - use comparisons of scale, and check them;
  - state the conditions behind speed-up claims ("a million times faster than…what, on which task?").

## Hype red flags (Intemann 2020)

Hype is exaggeration that misleads about how significant, certain, ready or beneficial research is, compared with what the evidence supports.

**Red flags:**
- Applications or timelines that go beyond what the authors claim.
- Benefits shown without risks, costs or downsides.
- "First" or "breakthrough" without the context of earlier work.
- Lab or simulation results presented as real-world performance.
- A single study presented as settled.
- A result that seems to overturn established physics, without independent confirmation. Such results are rare, and most turn out to be errors or refinements; the 2011 faster-than-light neutrinos were an equipment fault.
- Hype words: see the list in `press-and-media.md`.

**Fix:** state what was shown, under which conditions, and what remains to be done. Where possible, let an independent expert put it in context.

**Also:**
- **Your own sources can be inflated.** Researchers sometimes use hype strategically: in focus groups with 24 quantum physicists at one research centre, most said they did so, mainly for funding (Soto-Sanfiel et al. 2025). Calibrate their claims too. Ask what they would tell a colleague about timelines, and show them the headline and key claim before the full draft.
- **Name the hype** when others have overstated a result: "You may have seen headlines claiming X. Here is what the study showed." Quote the outlet's wording once, then say specifically what is accurate (which task, which conditions).
- **Don't overcorrect into dismissiveness.** If a result is genuinely significant, say so clearly and explain why.

## Responding to misinformation

**When to act:** when a false claim is spreading among your audience, or when it is harmful. Skip a myth that is neither spreading nor harmful. If few have heard it, publish your own account and monitor; if you expect it, prebunk. Backfire is rare: never hold back a needed correction, and repeat it, because corrections fade.

**Debunk** (Debunking Handbook 2020), in this order:
1. **Fact.** Lead with the correct fact, if it is simple and memorable; if it isn't, open with why the myth is wrong.
2. **Myth.** Warn that a myth follows, and mention it once only.
3. **Fallacy.** Explain why the myth seemed right, why it is wrong, and the flaw (cherry-picking, a single cause).
4. **Fact.** Repeat the correct fact.

Keep it short and plain. Give an alternative explanation that fills the gap the myth leaves, add a visual, use messengers this audience trusts on this topic, and never ridicule or label people ("deniers", "anti-science").

Put the correction right next to the myth and make it at least as prominent: in the headline, not buried. Never post the myth as a bare question. Where a source has a documented financial or political stake, say so factually.

**Prebunk** (Practical Guide to Prebunking, Harjani et al. 2022):
- A warning, the technique explained, and a weakened example (never the real myth).
- Common techniques: impersonation, emotional manipulation, polarisation, conspiratorial framing, ad hominem attacks, false balance, false dichotomies, scapegoating.
- Target a specific technique or claim: generic "beware of fake news" warnings can raise distrust of true information (COALESCE 2025, citing Hoes et al. 2024). Show people how to check a claim rather than asking them to trust science.
- Evidence: in a YouTube field study, 90-second videos raised recognition of a technique by about 5 percentage points (Roozenbeek et al. 2022). In another study the effect lasted about 10 days, and a 30-second booster extended it to at least 30 (Harjani et al. 2022).

**Conspiracy theories** (Conspiracy Theory Handbook 2020):
- Committed believers are rarely persuaded; speak to the undecided audience.
- General public: prebunk the warning signs, and debunk with facts.
- Committed believers: empathy, respect for their wish to think critically, no ridicule.
- If the theory targets your institution, acknowledge any real grievance, show that decisions follow fair procedures, and don't repeat the theory.
- Explain how science corrects itself (Sense about Science 2013).

**When the dispute is really about values or costs,** winning the uncertainty argument changes little: name the trade-off and the options (see `policy.md`).

**Online:** reply for the people reading along, link to sources, and don't let trolls set the agenda (safety advice is in `social-media.md`).

## Trust: what recent data say

- **68 countries, 71 922 people (Cologna et al. 2025):**
  - In most countries, most people trust scientists.
  - 83% agree that scientists should communicate about science with the general public.
  - About half (52%) want scientists to be more involved in policymaking.
  - In some European and North American countries, right-leaning people trust scientists less; in most countries there is no link.
- **EU (Special Eurobarometer 557, 2025):**
  - 83% see the overall influence of science and technology as positive.
  - 58% want to learn more about scientific developments.
  - 53% say science is too complicated to understand much of it.
  - Incorrect answers to factual and conspiracy statements increased since 2021.
- **Netherlands (Rathenau Instituut 2025):** average trust in science is high, about 7.5 out of 10, but more polarised than before.

**Implications:**
- Openness about funding, interests and uncertainty builds integrity.
- People rate scientists' competence highest and their openness to others' views lowest: show that you listen and how input is used.
- Show concretely who benefits and how (never invent the link): perceived benefit to "people like me" tracks trust more closely than science literacy does.
