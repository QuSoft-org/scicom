# Policy communication

## Choose your role (Pielke, *The Honest Broker*)

Pielke describes four idealised roles for experts in policy:

| Role | What you do | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Pure scientist | share knowledge without linking it to a decision | values are shared and uncertainty is low, and you are not answering decision-makers' questions |
| Science arbiter | answer decision-makers' factual questions, without recommending | values are shared and uncertainty is low |
| Issue advocate | argue for one option, narrowing the choice | values are contested or uncertainty is high, and you want to narrow the choice |
| Honest broker of policy alternatives | set out, or widen, the options and their consequences, and leave the choice to the decision-makers | values are contested or uncertainty is high, and you want to widen the choice |

**Diagnose the context in two steps:**
1. Are values (the goals) broadly shared, *and* is the uncertainty low? If both, act as a pure scientist or an arbiter. If either fails, act as an honest broker or an open advocate.
2. Then choose within the pair. Pure scientist or arbiter: are you answering the decision-makers' questions? Advocate or honest broker: do you want to narrow the choice or widen it?

- **Both yes:** Pielke calls this "tornado politics". Everyone wants to survive the tornado, and the only question is whether to stay or go. The facts settle it, so answering questions as an arbiter is enough.
- **Values conflict:** Pielke calls this "abortion politics". Science cannot settle the matter, and evidence is easily used as a proxy in a fight about values. Act as an honest broker, or as an openly declared advocate.
- **Shared goals, but high uncertainty** (common for technology risks and timing questions): the facts alone cannot settle it either. Pielke allows either honest broker or advocate here. Add robust or no-regrets options (steps that make sense across the plausible range of outcomes) to the set. If asked for a recommendation, give it, labelled as advocacy.
- **Complex system or deep uncertainty:** offer several adaptive options with review points, a range and a worst case.

**Further rules:**
- **Avoid stealth advocacy:** claiming to present "just the science" while steering towards a preferred policy. It is often unwitting. Warning sign: a disagreement about values presented as if the facts will settle it.
- **Say which role you are playing.** You can argue for a goal and still set out the options for reaching it. Recommending one option while openly comparing the others is advocacy, and fine when labelled. What you cannot do is claim to widen a choice while narrowing it. In close, trusted engagement the roles blur, so label each statement as evidence or as your judgement.
- **Honest brokering often works best as a group effort:** panels, committees, multidisciplinary teams.
- **Advocacy is legitimate:** Pielke himself calls open advocacy legitimate; his target is hidden advocacy. Don't use "advocate" to dismiss people; judge evidence on its merits (Rosenberg 2007).
- **Role check** before a policy contact or public statement (after Pielke and the Swiss Academies' 2025 roles guide):
  - Which role am I taking?
  - Who mandated or funded me, and must I say so?
  - Is a role being ascribed to me (activist, industry voice)?
  - What evidence would change this position?
  - Which other options fit the same evidence?

## What the advice says (Oliver & Cairney 2019; Cairney & Kwiatkowski 2017)

This is consensus practitioner advice, largely untested; don't present it as proven.

- **Relevant, high-quality research,** made accessible: short, plain and visual.
- **Know the policy process:**
  - who decides, when, and in which venue: ministry, parliament, agency, municipality, EU;
  - what is on their agenda;
  - windows of opportunity are rare: have the option worked out before one opens.
- **Frame evidence around policymakers' goals and problems,** not around your field's questions. Show what is within their power (Besley & Dudo). Use stories backed by evidence. Policymakers, like everyone, rely on shortcuts: beliefs, emotions, trusted sources.
- **Build relationships before you need them.** Be available, responsive and reliable. Work with intermediaries: advisory bodies, civil servants, learned societies, NGOs. Build trust first, bring challenging evidence second. Don't open with public criticism: a policymaker who has taken a public position finds it hard to reverse.
- **Who to approach:** check first with the institution's public affairs office. Aim for advisers and civil servants (in the Netherlands, beleidsmedewerkers and fractiemedewerkers) rather than the most senior person. Regional and local processes are easier to reach.
- **Who you write for:**
  - politicians and senior officials: short, actionable points, and what it means for them and their voters now;
  - civil servants, who may draft the rules: more context, and the clearest bottom line the evidence allows;
  - local authorities: what it means for their area.
  
  Break the data down to their region or remit, and bring options rather than only problems.
- **Be timely:** contribute while the decision is still being shaped. Early in a file, detailed input is welcome; late, give only short data that can go straight into the text.
- **Be clear about uncertainty,** and about what the evidence cannot tell you.
- **Know your limits.** Say where your expertise ends: certainty on one part doesn't carry over to the rest. You may decline a request to back a conclusion already reached.
- **Be patient and persistent.** Influence is rarely quick or linear. Engagement costs time and has risks: peer criticism, being used to legitimise a decision already taken, and abuse, which hits early-career researchers and women harder (see researcher safety in `ethics-integrity-ai.md`).
- **Wording:** "evidence-informed policy", not "evidence-based policy". Policy also weighs values.
- **Watch how the institute's research is used** in debate, and respond constructively when it is misquoted.

## Policy brief (usually 1–4 pages)

A one-pager gives talking points and one figure; a full review of options can run to 8 pages. Ask the recipient.

1. **Title:** the key finding or implication, in at most ten words.
2. **Key messages:** at most three findings, plus one line per option (at most three). Include the uncertainty that matters for the decision.
3. **The issue:** why it matters now, and for whom.
4. **What the evidence shows:** plain language, one or two visuals, and how certain it is. Lead with the weight of evidence, not the newest paper: novelty is not a criterion here. Cover the counterarguments the reader will meet.
5. **Options** (honest broker) or **recommendations** (advocate), each with consequences, costs and trade-offs.
   - Say whether the evidence supports, contradicts or is silent on each, and compare with the status quo.
   - State the criteria used to compare them; by default cost, feasibility, effectiveness, side effects and equity.
   - Make recommendations precise and feasible: who does what, by when. If the evidence can't separate the options, say so.
6. **Implications and next steps.** Don't default to "more research is needed".
7. **Sources, authors, contact details and date** ("based on evidence available on…"), whether it was peer reviewed, and a statement of interests. If an author has a commercial stake in a recommendation, declare it next to that recommendation and ask an independent reviewer to check it.

State the main caveat once up front, and keep it wherever the claim recurs.

- **Review:** co-producers shape the brief. Stakeholders with an interest check facts only, not the conclusions. Set a deadline, and have one non-expert read it. Time it to the policy agenda, and consider a public version.
- **The brief is not the strategy:** supplying briefs alone has not been shown to change policy. Send it to a short list, follow up personally and offer a meeting.
- **The position comes from the experts.** Draft the structure and the questions; never invent the group's assessment. If a starting position helps, mark it `[DRAFT POSITION for the authors to confirm or replace]`.
- **Ask early:**
  - question the question: the goal behind it, the scope, and what wasn't asked;
  - whether they need the state of knowledge, scenarios or routes to a goal already set; agree a sharper question and your role before you start;
  - which decision the brief feeds, by when, and for which systems or people;
  - whether it is confidential;
  - which language it should be in (Dutch ministries usually expect Dutch).

## Other policy formats

- **Briefing note:** one page with the issue, the evidence, the options and a contact.
- **Five-minute verbal briefing:** start with the bottom line; three points; one memorable number; offer follow-up; leave the one-pager behind.
- **Consultation response:** answer the questions actually asked, give evidence with references, and declare interests. Use a constructive tone, not a peer reviewer's.
- **Parliamentary hearing or round table:**
  - ask the organisers the purpose and who else speaks;
  - a short written statement in advance, with a summary on top;
  - a three-minute opening;
  - direct answers without jargon;
  - an offer to follow up in writing.
- **Expert panels and advisory reports:** keep evidence and judgement apart, and show uncertainty. Show majority and minority positions with their reasons; don't blend them into a vague middle. Name the assumptions and values that went in. In the public version, say why the other options were not chosen. Members giving interviews speak in a personal capacity, say so, and keep deliberations confidential unless the panel agreed otherwise.

## EU science-for-policy notes (JRC *Science for Policy Handbook*; SAPEA 2019)

- In EU advisory work, the default is to set out options and the goals each serves, and to give your view only when asked.
- How much certainty is enough to act is the decision-maker's call. State how robust the evidence is and what being wrong would cost in each direction, and name the precautionary principle where it applies.
- Co-create the question with policymakers, and understand where the decision stands.
- **No surprises, no veto:** before publishing results that challenge a government's line, warn the policymakers involved. In commissioned work, agree in writing at the start what may be published. Advance notice never means changing the findings. For sensitive results, have the press office and public affairs read the text.
