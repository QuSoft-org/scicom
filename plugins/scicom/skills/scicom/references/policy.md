# Policy communication

## Choose your role (Pielke, *The Honest Broker*)

Pielke describes four idealised roles for experts in policy:

| Role | What you do | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Pure scientist | share knowledge without linking it to a decision | there is no decision at stake |
| Science arbiter | answer decision-makers' factual questions, without recommending | values are shared and uncertainty is low |
| Issue advocate | argue for one option, narrowing the choice | you are openly committed to an outcome |
| Honest broker of policy alternatives | set out, or widen, the options and their consequences, and leave the choice to the decision-makers | values are contested and/or uncertainty is high |

**Diagnose the context with two questions:**
1. Is there broad agreement on values, that is, on the goals?
2. Is the uncertainty low?

- **Both yes:** Pielke calls this "tornado politics". Everyone wants to survive the tornado, and the only question is whether to stay or go. The facts settle it, so answering questions as an arbiter is enough.
- **Values conflict:** Pielke calls this "abortion politics". Science cannot settle the matter, and evidence is easily used as a proxy in a fight about values. Act as an honest broker, or as an openly declared advocate.
- **Shared goals, but high uncertainty** (common for technology risks and timing questions): the facts alone cannot settle it either.
  - Offer options with their consequences, as an honest broker.
  - If the policymaker explicitly asks for advice, give a clearly labelled recommendation of robust actions: steps that make sense across the plausible range of outcomes.
  - Keep evidence and judgement visibly apart.

**Further rules:**
- **Avoid stealth advocacy:** claiming to present "just the science" while steering towards a preferred policy.
- **Say which role you are playing.** You cannot widen and narrow the options at the same time.
- **Honest brokering often works best as a group effort:** panels, committees, multidisciplinary teams.
- **Critics' point:** transparent advocacy is legitimate and can help policy. What matters is being open about it.
- **More roles:** the Swiss Academies' 2025 guide adds roles such as knowledge broker, facilitator and change agent, with questions for reflecting on your own role.

## What actually helps (Oliver & Cairney 2019; Cairney & Kwiatkowski 2017)

- **Relevant, high-quality research,** made accessible: short, plain and visual.
- **Know the policy process:**
  - who decides, when, and in which venue: ministry, parliament, agency, municipality, EU;
  - what is on their agenda;
  - windows of opportunity open and close.
- **Frame evidence around policymakers' goals and problems,** not around your field's questions. Show what is within their power. Use stories backed by evidence. Policymakers, like everyone, rely on shortcuts: beliefs, emotions, trusted sources.
- **Build relationships before you need them.** Be available, responsive and reliable. Work with intermediaries: advisory bodies, civil servants, learned societies, NGOs.
- **Who to approach:** check first with the institution's public affairs office. Aim for advisers and civil servants (in the Netherlands, beleidsmedewerkers and fractiemedewerkers) rather than the most senior person. Regional and local processes are easier to reach.
- **Who you write for:** elected officials think about constituents; civil servants may draft the rules. Break the data down to their region or remit, and bring options rather than only problems.
- **Be timely:** contribute while the decision is still being shaped.
- **Be clear about uncertainty,** and about what the evidence cannot tell you.
- **Be patient and persistent.** Influence is rarely quick or linear, and engagement costs time and can cost reputation.
- **Pick your role** (see above) and stay consistent.

## Policy brief (2–4 pages)

Budget about 400–450 words per page with one visual, so a two-page brief runs roughly 800–900 words.

1. **Title:** the key finding or implication.
2. **Key messages:** three to five bullets at the top.
3. **The issue:** why it matters now, and for whom.
4. **What the evidence shows:** plain language, one or two visuals, and how certain it is.
5. **Options** (honest broker) or **recommendations** (advocate), each with consequences, costs and trade-offs. State the criteria used to compare them. Make recommendations precise and feasible.
6. **Implications and next steps.**
7. **Sources, authors, contact details and date.**

Share a draft with intended users, co-produce it where possible, and time it to the policy agenda. Consider a public version.

- **The position comes from the experts.** Draft the structure and the questions; never invent the group's assessment. If a starting position helps, mark it `[DRAFT POSITION for the authors to confirm or replace]`.
- **Ask early:**
  - which decision the brief feeds, by when, and for which systems or people;
  - whether it is confidential;
  - which language it should be in (Dutch ministries usually expect Dutch).

## Other policy formats

- **Briefing note:** one page with the issue, the evidence, the options and a contact.
- **Five-minute verbal briefing:** start with the bottom line; three points; one memorable number; offer follow-up; leave the one-pager behind.
- **Consultation response:** answer the questions actually asked, give evidence with references, and declare interests.
- **Parliamentary hearing or round table:**
  - ask the organisers the purpose and who else speaks;
  - a short written statement in advance, with a summary on top;
  - a three-minute opening;
  - direct answers without jargon;
  - an offer to follow up in writing.
- **Expert panels and advisory reports:** keep evidence and judgement apart, and show uncertainty and dissenting views.

## EU science-for-policy notes (JRC *Science for Policy Handbook*; SAPEA 2019)

- Advice informs rather than prescribes. Make uncertainty, complexity and value choices visible.
- Co-create the question with policymakers, and understand where the policy cycle stands.
- Work through networks and communities; involve citizens where relevant.
- Communicate early and in plain language.
