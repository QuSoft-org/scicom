# Press, news and media

## Pick the right format

| Format | For | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| News item (website/newsroom) | Broad audience: staff, students, partners, funders, the public | Most papers, grants, events and appointments. 300–800 words, more context and people than a release, no embargo needed |
| Press release | Journalists | Only for real news (see the test below). Coordinated with the journal embargo; includes contacts and media assets |
| News tip | A few selected journalists | Smaller or niche stories. Three to five sentences, link to the paper |
| Media advisory | Journalists, photographers | Inviting them to an event or photo opportunity: what, who, when, where, why, access, contact |
| Personal pitch | One journalist | A tailored offer, possibly an exclusive |
| Expert availability | Journalists covering a topical story | When a topic is in the news and your researcher can explain it |
| Press conference | Journalists, camera crews | Rarely: a live demonstration, a high-level guest or several partners speaking. Otherwise an embargoed release |

## Is it news? Test before writing a press release

First, it must be **credible**: peer-reviewed, or with its status clearly labelled (preprint; conference abstract or talk). For an abstract-only result, a pitch or news item usually fits better than a release. Then count how many apply:
- **New:** a real step, not an incremental one.
- **Significant:** for the field, or for people's lives.
- **Relevant** to the outlet's audience.
- **Timely:** publication, event, anniversary, or a link to current news.
- **Human interest:** people, surprise, stakes.
- **Visual:** striking images or video exist.
- **Local:** a regional or national angle.
- **Exclusive:** offered to one outlet first.
- **Debate in the field:** it challenges or settles a disagreement.
- **Follow-up:** a development in a running story.

Rule of thumb: if fewer than three apply, write a news item and social posts instead. Over-releasing teaches journalists to ignore your releases.

## Press release structure (350–700 words of body text, headline to limitations)

Extra detail goes in a linked background note or fact sheet.

1. **Labels** at the top, adapted from the AMS/SMC labelling system (UK, for medical research). Choose them with the researcher, not from the paper's self-description:
   - status: peer-reviewed / preprint (not peer-reviewed) / conference: full paper refereed / conference: abstract, poster or talk (not peer-reviewed);
   - type: theory, simulation, lab experiment, observational study, trial, prototype, review;
   - subject: people, animals, cells, materials, devices, models, data.
2. **Headline:** the finding in plain words with an active verb, about 12 words at most. No question, no pun that hides the meaning, no hype. If the results are mixed, the headline says so. Put the main condition (lab only, simulation, small sample) in the headline, subhead or lede.
3. **Subhead:** one sentence on why it matters, or key context.
4. **Embargo line,** placed at the very top, above the labels and headline: `EMBARGOED UNTIL 16:00 CET, 4 March 2027`, matching the journal's embargo exactly. For a midnight embargo write 00:01; use CET or CEST as applies. Don't write "for immediate release" on research stories.
5. **Lede** (about 35 words at most): who found what, and why it matters.
6. **Context:** the problem, what was known before, who else works on it, and whether this confirms or contradicts earlier work.
7. **How they did it:** the method in plain words, with one concrete, vivid detail.
8. **Quote from the lead researcher:** meaning, motivation or significance, not a repeat of the facts. It must be real and approved. If none is supplied, use a `[QUOTE: …]` placeholder (see the quote rule in `SKILL.md`). Quotes follow the same calibration as the text: a hype word in quotation marks is still hype.
9. **Implications and next steps:** realistic, with anything speculative marked as such.
10. **Limitations:** conditions, stage, and what the work does not show. For observational work, say early in the text what the design cannot show and what was controlled for. Not "more research is needed".
11. **Optional second quote** from a co-author or partner who agreed to it, labelled as such. Name independent experts journalists can call in the notes to editors, not in the release.
12. **Credits:** institutions, collaborators, funders, plus any required acknowledgement text. Competing interests: spin-off shares, patents, industry funding.
13. **Reference:** title, journal, DOI or preprint link, publication date.
14. **Media contacts:** name, phone, email, plus the researcher's direct contact if they agree and the house style allows. Name the first and the senior author. Say when the researcher is available, and in which languages.
15. **Media assets:** images and video with caption, credit, licence and alt text, plus a download link.
16. **Notes to editors:** the organisation's boilerplate.

In your delivery notes (not for distribution), add a **source map**: each number, comparison and caption with its place in the paper, or "confirmed by researcher". Fact-checkers go line by line.

## Calibrate the claims

| Evidence | Write | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Correlational / observational | "is associated with", "is linked to", "may" + verb | "can" + verb, "causes", "reduces", "boosts" |
| Experimental (controlled trial or lab experiment) | direct verbs are fine | over-hedging a well-controlled result |
| Lab, cells, animals, model systems, simulations | Name the system in the headline or lede: "in mice", "in a lab prototype", "in simulations" | Implying it works in people or in the real world |
| Theory | "shows theoretically", "predicts"; "proves" only for mathematical proofs | "proves" for anything empirical |
| Prototype / proof of principle | "demonstrates in the lab", "a first step towards" | "paves the way", "will enable" |
| Preprint | "in a study not yet peer reviewed" | Presenting it as established |
| Possible applications | What would still need to happen, and who says so | Years or dates, unless the researchers give and justify them |
| Advice or recommendations | no stronger than the paper's own | adding "should", including in quotes |

**Researcher sign-off does not prevent exaggeration.** Besides facts and quotes, ask them to check the headline and first two sentences against the results.

**Hype words to justify or delete:**
- breakthrough, unprecedented, revolutionary, game-changer, holy grail;
- first (check it!), transform, unlock, crack, solve, cure;
- prove (outside mathematics), mystery solved, paves the way, could lead to.

**Why it matters** (UK and Dutch health research: Sumner et al. 2014; Sumner et al. 2016; Adams et al. 2019; Bossema et al. 2019):
- When releases exaggerated advice, causation or animal-to-human relevance, the news often did too (for advice, about half the time). When releases were accurate, the news mostly was as well.
- Caveats, and claims aligned with the evidence, did not reduce news uptake.
- Only about 7% of UK and Dutch health stories added an outside expert. Journalists mostly use what the release gives them, so offer experts they can call.

**Health and medical research needs extra care:**
- Never suggest a treatment or cure from studies in cells, animals or early human trials. Name the stage in the headline or lede.
- Avoid false hope for patients and families: say what it does not mean yet, and how far off any treatment is, if the researchers say so.
- Use the press-release labels, and consider asking a patient organisation or independent clinician for context.
- Never give medical advice. Where relevant, point readers to their doctor. If a finding concerns a medicine or device people already use, put the absolute numbers and "don't stop without talking to your doctor" near the top, not only in spoken remarks.

## Approval and embargoes

- **The researcher** checks facts, claims and quotes. The researcher decides on facts and claims, the communicator on structure and wording: discuss any discomfort about wording, and fix every factual concern.
- **Several reviewers:** agree the outline (message, angle, main claim, headline) before drafting; merge comments yourself; keep accuracy fixes, and resist edits that add hype or jargon. Interview researchers and draft the lay text yourself rather than asking them to write it. Push back on publicity for unreviewed work or a project in trouble: tie it to a publication or a clearly labelled stage.
- **Early warning:** researchers tell the press office at the minor-revisions stage, or when a conference abstract is accepted.
- **Partners and co-authors:** agree who leads, the timing, the quotes and who pitches which journalist. Partner press offices may want their own version; share drafts early.
- **Funders:** inform them, and use the required acknowledgement text and logos.
- **Journal embargoes:** follow the journal's policy, including time and time zone. Never share embargoed results publicly before it lifts. Send embargoed material only to journalists who accept the embargo, or via EurekAlert! or AlphaGalileo, and give them access to the paper. If the embargo breaks, lift it for the whole list at once and tell them.
- **Already public:** a preprint on arXiv cannot be embargoed. Say so, and embargo only the release and any new material. Before interviews, tell the researcher that anything in the preprint can be reported now.
- **Preprints** are common in physics, mathematics and computer science. Say when a result is a preprint, and check the target journal's policy before publicising a submitted paper.
- **Patents:** check with the technology transfer office before any public disclosure.

## Distribution and timing

- **Channels:**
  - your own newsroom and social media;
  - targeted journalists (science desks, trade press, local media);
  - the newsletters, society magazines and trade sites the researcher actually reads;
  - EurekAlert! and AlphaGalileo for international science journalists;
  - the national news agency;
  - partner and funder channels.
- **Timing:**
  - send under embargo two to five days ahead, so journalists can prepare and interview;
  - avoid holidays, and avoid clashing with your own other big news;
  - make sure the researcher is reachable when the embargo lifts;
  - promote conference papers about a week before the meeting;
  - prepare backgrounders, diagrams and an expert list for predictable events in your field (launches, anniversaries, recurring hazards);
  - when your topic breaks in the news, offer expertise within about a day, with a few short points, the context (compared with last time? a trend? causes?) and how sure the estimate is;
  - a paper that came out weeks ago gets a news item, not a release, unless it can be tied to a policy moment (a vote, a consultation deadline) with an angle the researcher agrees to.
- **Sending:** paste the release into the email body; subject line = headline; recipients in BCC.
- **Follow-up:** don't chase a mass release; follow up only with something new for that journalist. (A personal pitch gets one follow-up; see below.) Track coverage and log the results.

## Pitching a journalist

- **Do your homework:** read what they cover first.
- **Subject line** = the news.
- **Opening:** the first two sentences say what it is and why *their* readers will care. Then: why now, and what you offer (an interview, data, visuals, a lab visit).
- **Limitation up front:** state the main one ("lab only so far").
- **Length and format:** 100–150 words; a link rather than attachments.
- **Follow up** once, after two or three working days.
- **Exclusives:** offer to one outlet at a time, with agreed timing.

A template is in `templates.md`.

## Grants, awards and appointments

- Lead with what the money or the role makes possible, and why it matters. Then who, how much and from whom.
- Skip "proud to announce" and "happy to share".
- Announce partnerships and MoUs only when the benefits are concrete (money, equipment, named projects). Never appear to endorse a partner's product.
- Name the funder and programme correctly.
- Usually this is news for the newsroom, social media and the trade press, not for general journalists. Large, unusual or local-angle grants are the exception.

## Preparing a researcher for an interview

**Before:**
- Find out: outlet, audience, angle, other sources, format (live or recorded; studio, phone or video), length and deadline.
- **Spokesperson:** the one who explains well and has time, not necessarily the most senior.
- **Live talk shows with other guests:** ask who else is on and what they will say; agree the one point to land; there is no chance to correct afterwards; don't debate a sceptic as an equal on settled points.
- **Unexpected call:** ask the outlet, angle and deadline, and agree a time to call back, even in ten minutes. Note two or three key points first.
- **Disclose relationships:** list the researcher's industry partners, spin-offs, board roles and advisory committee seats, and mention them proactively. Suggest independent experts, including likely critics.
- **False balance:** prepare one line on where the weight of evidence lies and how large any dissent is.
- **Policy questions:** decide beforehand how to answer "What should the government do?": the options and trade-offs, or a personal view labelled as such (see `policy.md`).
- Prepare one main message, two supporting points, one concrete example or analogy, and one memorable number.
- Prepare for the hard questions: limitations, applications and timelines, money, risks, controversy, and "what does this mean for me?". Also: how does this compare with other studies? Is it strong enough to change anything now? For "Can you rule it out?" or "Is it safe?", give the strongest honest statement ("the evidence gives us high confidence that…"), not a refusal.
- Practise aloud, with a fluent speaker if the interview is not in their first language. Answers of two or three sentences (20–30 seconds) that make sense on their own, because the question may be cut. For recorded radio and TV, prepare 5–10-second soundbites that contain the question.

**During:**
- Everything is on the record, including before and after the recording, and email or chat replies. In the Netherlands, anyone taking part in a call may record it without telling the others; elsewhere the rules differ, so check local law. Either way, assume you are being recorded. Avoid "off the record".
- Start with the conclusion, then explain.
- Answer the question, then bridge back to the message.
- Stay within your expertise. "I don't know" is fine; "no comment" is not. Explain why you cannot answer.
- Calmly correct misunderstandings. No jargon, and round the numbers.
- Don't fill silences. Decline hypothetical questions. On contested topics, record the interview yourself.
- Credit colleagues by name inside an answer: news stories have no acknowledgements.

**Radio, TV and video:**
- **Nobody checks the facts after the recording.** Send the producer a one-page fact sheet beforehand (names and spellings, the finding in one line, the main caveat, key numbers with units), and restate the key number and caveat at the end. A documentary or podcast fact-checker is a fact check, not a chance to rephrase: answer fast.
- Arrive early. Look at the interviewer, not the camera (unless remote). Assume the microphone is live from the moment it is fitted.
- Plain clothes: no stripes, small patterns, logos, or pure black or white. Sit upright.
- Remotely: light in front of you, a headset microphone, a neutral background, the camera at eye level.

**After:**
- Always ask to check your own quotes; this is a courtesy, not a right, and gives no veto. In the Netherlands, expect quotes to be used in the expected context, and to be asked again if the piece changes. Ask to read the rest for factual accuracy only: "May I read the piece to check for factual errors?" (in Dutch: "Mag ik het stuk lezen om te controleren op feitelijke onjuistheden?"). Agree this when the interview is set up. Correct facts; don't ask to change the angle, tone or wording. If the outlet won't share text, email the key points, numbers and spellings, and ask them to read the sentence describing the finding back to you.
- Thank them, share the coverage, log it, and flag errors.

## Corrections and hostile coverage

- **Factual error:** if it would mislead readers, contact the journalist politely: the article and date, the error, the correct fact and a source. Go to the editor only if needed. Separate errors from interpretations you dislike. Ask for the fix the format allows: a correction note online, updated audio plus a note, a note in the next issue. A missing key caveat is valid ground too; ask for an update note, not removal. For Dutch outlets the last resort is a complaint to the Raad voor de Journalistiek.
- **Your own errors:** correct your news items visibly, with a dated note; never edit silently. If a paper you promoted is corrected or retracted, add a dated note to the original item.
- **Critical story:** journalists send the main points and a deadline. Reply in time, even with a holding line, or the story will say you did not respond. Ask for the claims in writing.
- **Misleading coverage elsewhere:** post a short, factual clarification on your own channels. Don't amplify fringe pieces.
- **Hostile interview:** stick to facts and stay calm. You may end an interview that turns abusive. Inform the press office, and see researcher safety in `ethics-integrity-ai.md`.

## Crisis communication for an institution

Principles from the CDC's crisis and emergency risk communication (CERC) guidance: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action, show respect. The steps below combine CERC and practice.

**Prepare beforehand:** draft statements for known risk areas, such as contested research, animal work, dual-use, data breaches and lab incidents. Agree a sign-off route that works within hours (one scientist for facts, one manager); name and train the spokesperson. When the statement goes out, give reception and partner press offices a short Q&A.

1. **Gather facts** and form a small team: communications, management, legal, HR, and the researcher if relevant. If legal advice conflicts with openness, flag it to management.
2. **Holding statement within hours:** what we know, what is not yet known (labelled as such), what we are doing, when we will update. Date-stamp each update. Say who is at risk and what the risk is, in plain words, and what people can do. Show empathy if people are affected.
3. **Disclose at once,** not in instalments. Own the institution's faults in the active voice ("we made a mistake"), apologise, and say what will change. Never deny something true.
4. **One spokesperson**, briefed; for serious harm, the head of the institution. Inform staff before or at the same time as the outside world. No paternalistic tone; no public disagreements between spokespeople.
5. **No speculation.** Don't blame individuals or third parties before the facts are in. Correct rumours with facts.
6. **Update on a regular rhythm** and document decisions. If coverage keeps missing a key point, repeat it plainly in every update.
7. **Review afterwards** and write down the lessons.

Typical university crises: lab incidents, research misconduct, controversial research, data breaches, threats to researchers, protests. Many are issues you can see coming (a misconduct outcome): plan the timing.
