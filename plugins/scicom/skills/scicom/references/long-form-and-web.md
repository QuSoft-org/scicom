# Long-form and web texts

## Op-eds (Goh & Bourne 2020; Dean 2009)

- **Argue, don't announce:** an op-ed argues, a press release informs. You need one argument you can state in a single sentence.
- **Hook it to the news:** link it to something happening now.
- **Structure:**
  1. The argument in the first two or three paragraphs.
  2. Two or three strong pieces of evidence.
  3. The strongest counter-argument, and your answer to it.
  4. A concrete call to action or recommendation.
- **Length and style:** 600–800 words, depending on the outlet. Plain language, one or two numbers, one personal or concrete example. First person where it fits.
- **Byline:** name, role, institution. Disclose relevant interests and funding.
- **Pitching:** offer to one outlet at a time (most want exclusivity), with a short summary. Follow up once. Have drafts ready before the hook arrives, and paste the text into the email body.
- **Press office:** tell them early, and check before using the institution's name.
- **Criticising a named person or organisation:** give them a chance to respond, and check with the press office or legal team.

## Letters to the editor

Reply within a day or two, in under about 150 words. Name the article and date, make one point, and give your role.

## Blogs and explainers

- **Title:** a question ("Why do quantum computers have to be so cold?"), answered in the first paragraph.
- **Then:** how we know, what is uncertain, and what comes next.
- **Format:** 600–1,200 words, with subheadings, links to sources and a figure.
- A personal voice is fine on a researcher's own blog.

## Features and profiles

- **Opening:** a scene, a person or a surprising detail.
- **Nut graf** by the third or fourth paragraph: what the story is about and why now.
- **Body:** alternate story and explanation, and let people speak, with approved quotes.
- **Ending:** a kicker that echoes the opening or looks ahead.
- **Length:** 1,000–2,500 words.

## Newsletters (The Online Scientist)

- Give readers something useful or interesting in every issue, not just announcements.
- **Layout:** a steady rhythm, short items, headings, a personal opening, and one main call to action.
- **Subject line:** specific about what is inside. No clickbait.

## Lay summaries

- **Length:** 150–250 words.
- **Four questions:** What was the question? What was done? What was found? Why does it matter, and what comes next?
- **Style:** no jargon, acronyms spelled out, technical numbers only where essential, and the limitations in one sentence.

## Web pages (research groups, projects, institutes)

- **Purpose:** say who the page is for and what they can do there.
- **Writing:** front-load the key information; headings; short paragraphs; plain language; visible dates, kept up to date.
- **Research group page:**
  - what we study and why it matters, and how;
  - who we are, with photos;
  - latest news;
  - how to collaborate or join;
  - contact details.
- **EU project websites:** write for the public and stakeholders, not only for the funder. Plan for archiving after the project ends.
- **Accessibility:** headings in order, alt text, enough contrast, descriptive link text, captioned video.

## Bios (The Online Scientist)

- **Order:** start with what you study and why it matters, then role and affiliation, two or three highlights, and a human touch if it fits, such as why they do the work.
- **Versions:** a short one (about 50 words) and a long one (about 150 words).
- **Person:** third person on institutional pages, first person on LinkedIn.

## Impact stories (annual reports, funders)

- **Structure:** the problem → what the research contributed → evidence of change (data, quotes from partners) → what comes next.
- **Precision:** be exact about contribution versus attribution, and credit partners.
