# Strategy, stakeholders and evaluation

Use this file for communication plans (for a paper, project, grant, event or new institute), campaigns, audience choices, stakeholder analyses and evaluation.

## The strategy chain (after Besley & Dudo)

1. **Organisational goal.** Examples: recruit international PhD candidates, secure follow-up funding, inform a policy debate, build trust with neighbours, attract industry partners, become visible in the field.
2. **Behavioural goal: what should a specific group do?**
   - Examples: "physics master's students apply to our PhD programme"; "ministry officials use our evidence when drafting the strategy"; "local residents come to the open day and ask questions".
   - Broader aims count too: seeing science as legitimate, accepting new findings, supporting research funding.
   - Include what you are willing to change yourself: listening, adapting plans or research questions.
3. **Communication objectives:** the beliefs, feelings and frames that make that behaviour more likely. Pick one to three where the audience has a real gap. For trust, name the missing dimension.

| Objective | The audience believes or feels… | What to put in the text |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | understands the key facts | only what the goal needs; ask first what they believe. Needed, but rarely enough on its own |
| Warmth | you want to help and care about them | one first-person line on why this problem, and whom it helps |
| Integrity | you are honest and fair | the funder and what it controls; peer-review status; open data; a setback or limitation |
| Willingness to listen | you take their views seriously | explain the process before the decision; say how input is used; report back |
| Shared values | you are like them, or respect their differences | messengers, photos and places the audience recognises; common ground first |
| Competence | you know what you are doing | plain words, one meaningful number, credible partners |
| Risks and benefits | the trade-offs are clear and fair | a specific benefit (for whom, when, how sure); who bears the risks; with any threat, a fix that works |
| Norms | people like them do this | a real share of a group they identify with |
| Self-efficacy | they can do it | the step, how long it takes, someone similar doing it; never overstate how easy it is |
| Emotions and frames | the honest feeling and a fitting interpretation | the researcher's own feeling, in the first person; enjoyment and humour are fine; no engineered fear or shame. Name the frame (social progress, economic opportunity, ethics, public accountability) and why it fits their values |

4. **Tactics:** message, messenger, tone, channel, timing and format.
5. **Evaluation:** did the tactic shift the objective, and is that objective actually linked to the behaviour?

**Worksheet questions for each objective** (paraphrased from Besley & Dudo's appendix):
- Why would this belief help reach the goal?
- How far does the audience already hold it? Is there room for change?
- What could we ethically do to strengthen it?
- How will we know whether it changed?
- Is it honest to want them to believe this? Only pursue beliefs that are true of you.
- The reverse view: what do we believe about this audience, and how can we find out more?

## Stakeholder map for universities and research institutes

| Group | Typically wants | We may want | Channels | Tone and formats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Researchers and PhD candidates (internal) | recognition, support, simple processes | early tips on news; spokespeople | intranet, chat channels, meetings | collegial, practical |
| Students | relevance, opportunities | engagement, ambassadors | learning platform, social media, campus screens | informal, visual |
| Management and support staff | overview, reputation | support, resources | internal newsletters, reports | concise, factual |
| Peers, collaborators, consortia | substance, correct credit | collaboration, citations | conferences, LinkedIn, Bluesky, project sites | technical is fine; precise credit |
| Prospective students and PhD candidates (often international) | what it is like, people, careers | applications | website, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, fairs, open days | authentic, people-first |
| Prospective staff and postdocs | research environment, conditions | applications | LinkedIn, vacancy pages, Bluesky | honest, specific |
| Funders (research councils, EU/ERC, foundations, industry) | impact, accountability, visibility | renewed funding, trust | personal meetings, reports, events, tagged posts | evidence-based, correct acknowledgements |
| Policymakers, civil servants, regulators, advisory bodies | reliable, timely, concise evidence | evidence use, invitations to advise | briefs, meetings, hearings, LinkedIn | short, neutral, options (see `policy.md`) |
| Industry, start-ups, investors, innovation programmes, technology transfer | applications, talent, partnerships | partnerships, licences, internships | personal meetings, trade press, LinkedIn, events | concrete; honest about technology readiness |
| Journalists and creators | news, access, accuracy, speed | accurate coverage | releases, pitches, expert lists | see `press-and-media.md` |
| The public, by segment: sciencephiles, critically interested, passive supporters, disengaged | curiosity, relevance, reasons to trust | interest, trust, participation | TV and radio, social video, events, museums | plain, warm, visual |
| Local community and neighbours | effects on their area, safety, access | goodwill, participation | local media, open days, letters | respectful, two-way |
| Schools, teachers, pupils, parents | curriculum fit, role models, inspiration | interest in STEM, future students | school visits, lesson materials, festivals | age-appropriate, hands-on |
| NGOs, civil society, citizen scientists | relevance to their cause, a voice | dialogue, collaboration | meetings, co-creation | partnership |
| Underserved groups | access and respect | inclusion | trusted intermediaries | co-designed; never condescending |
| Alumni and donors | pride, connection, impact | support, ambassadors | personal contact, magazine, events, newsletter | stories |

## Prioritise

**Power–interest grid:**

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | keep satisfied | manage closely |
| **Low power** | monitor | keep informed |

- Also ask about **urgency** (who must hear first) and **legitimacy** (who has a rightful stake).
- **Quadruple-helix check:** academia, government, industry, civil society. Did we miss a whole sector?
- **Internal audiences come first:** researchers are your source of stories and your spokespeople.
  - Staff hear major news no later than the public, with the reason, not only the decision.
  - Recruit spokespeople by what they gain: visibility to collaborators, applicants and funders.

## Audience insight: ask before you write

- What do they already know and believe? What do they value?
- What is in it for them?
- Where do they get information?
  - In the EU, TV is still the main source of science news (61%), followed by social media and blogs (31%) (Special Eurobarometer 557, 2025).
  - Online news increasingly reaches people through social and video platforms (Digital News Report 2026).
- What stops them? Past experience, misconceptions, fear, habit. 53% of Europeans say science is so complicated they do not understand much of it (Eurobarometer 2025).
- Study the audience itself, not intermediaries: to reach students, ask students, not their teachers.
- Whom do they trust as a messenger?

## Planning tools

- **OASIS** (UK Government Communication Service): objectives, audience insight, strategy and ideas, implementation, scoring (evaluation). Template in `templates.md`.
- **Message Box** (COMPASS): issue, problem, so what, solutions, benefits. Fill it in with the researcher before any media work.
- **Plan for a paper or project:**
  - fact sheet;
  - existing assets: event series, visitable labs, channels, staff languages;
  - goal and audiences;
  - Message Box;
  - formats and channels;
  - timeline (embargo, launch, follow-up);
  - roles: who writes, approves and speaks;
  - risks: sensitivities, possible misreadings;
  - a pretest: show key materials to two or three people from the audience. What is the main point, what will you remember, what will you do?
  - indicators.
- **Communication for a new institute or group** (practitioner steps, The Online Scientist):
  1. Take stock of existing channels.
  2. Define the identity: a theory of change turned into a short narrative.
  3. Run a SWOT and benchmark comparable institutes.
  4. Choose priority audiences.
  5. For each output, decide goal, audience, message and medium.
  6. Set up the basics: slide, poster and visual templates; a simple website; social accounts; boilerplate.
  7. Build a network of partner press offices and journalists, and appoint spokespeople.
  8. Consider a professional communication adviser.
- **EU-funded projects:**
  - Keep three activities apart: communication (wide audiences, about the project), dissemination (results to those who can use them) and exploitation (putting results to use).
  - Follow the grant agreement's visibility rules: EU emblem and funding statement.
  - Agree a short glossary of terms that differ between disciplines before external communication starts.

## Do less, better

- Choose a few channels you can keep up rather than trying to be everywhere. Open a new site or account only if someone can post at least weekly for its whole life.
- Avoid visibility for its own sake. Competitive institutional PR can erode trust when promotion overrides accuracy (Weingart & Joubert 2019; Entradas et al. 2020).
- Not every paper needs a press release; see the test in `press-and-media.md`.

## Evaluation

- **Levels** (GCS Evaluation Cycle):
  - inputs;
  - outputs: what we produced;
  - outtakes: what people noticed and understood;
  - outcomes: what changed in attitudes or behaviour;
  - impact: long-term change.
- **Indicators:** pick one to three per objective. Measure a baseline where you can, and combine numbers with quotes and observations.
  - Outputs and outtakes: reach, engagement, accuracy of coverage (not just volume).
  - Outcomes: applications, enquiries, partnerships, citations in policy documents.
- **No advertising-value equivalents.**
- **Events:** a three-question survey (what did you learn, how did it feel, would you come back?), observation, and follow-up. Check delivery first: did the Q&A actually happen?
- **Don't overclaim:** a survey after an event shows experience, not attitude change. That needs two measurements against an objective.
- **Grant proposals:** name the metrics and an audience survey; never write just "we will post on social media".
- **Close the loop:** write down what worked, and stop what didn't.
