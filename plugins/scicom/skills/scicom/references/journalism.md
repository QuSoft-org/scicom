# Journalism (role: journalist)

Use this file when the user is a science journalist (see "Role" in `SKILL.md`). It says what Claude does for a journalist: what it flags, drafts, asks and refuses. The craft files (writing, uncertainty, visuals, review checklist) still apply. Rules that serve an institution as sender do not. Templates: `templates-journalism.md`.

## Starting point

- A press release is a lead to verify, never text to rewrite. Attribute release wording ("the university said"). Never turn a communicator-role draft (a release or news item, even one this user wrote for an institution) into journalist copy: report the story anew.
- Match before you repeat: confirm another outlet's scoop with your own source; if you can't, attribute it and say you could not confirm it ("as NRC first reported").
- Report a source's funding as context and as a possible conflict, not as an acknowledgement. When the story is about the institution itself (governance, misconduct, industry ties, safety), its press office is a party: use its statement as a reply, not as background.
- If the user mentions a conflict of their own (a paid trip, comms work for an institution they cover, shares) or of the outlet (a funded desk, a partnership), suggest flagging it to the editor and to readers. Sponsored or partner content is labelled and not written by the news journalist.

## Should we cover it?

- **Weak pegs:** it's trending, it's embargoed, the release says it's big.
- **Good reasons:** outside scientists are excited; it affects people; it explains something in the news; errors about it are spreading. Also ask how widespread and severe the problem is, and whether it is distinctive here.
- Ask "what is new, and how does it move the story on?". When one paper is weak, suggest a weight-of-evidence explainer or trend piece instead.
- **An unverified claim that is already public** (a viral preprint, a press-conference result): ask the claimant for evidence and others who could check; the story is how the field reacts. Straight lede, then the condition ("if confirmed…") and outside doubt within the first three paragraphs; follow the replication attempts. If it hasn't spread, verify and hold.
- **Everyday-hazard stories** (chemicals in tea bags, bacteria on phones): ask for exposure, dose and harm at realistic levels. If there is no harm at real exposure, advise a calm explainer or no story, not an exposé.

## Assessing a study or press release

Build the fact sheet (`SKILL.md` step 2), then check these red flags. The same list is the fill-in block in the template file.

- **Paper versus release and talk:** read the paper. Mark every claim in the release, headline, quote or press-conference remark that the paper doesn't support; researchers often go further when speaking.
- **Design and comparator:** what was tested against what? The best existing option, or a straw man ("faster than the best classical method?").
- **Sample:** who or what was studied, how many, how chosen (all devices or the best one; selected runs); dropouts or subgroups left out of the headline numbers; population and sex where relevant, and whom the result applies to.
- **Stage and venue, in words:** a plan (grant), work in progress, a conference abstract, a preprint, peer reviewed (the safest point to report, still not proof), a press conference first, a predatory journal. An exclusive on a big claim is itself a red flag: ask why nobody else has it.
- **Effect:** absolute size; "compared with what?"; practical, not just statistical, significance. For technology: where it has been shown (theory, lab, field), what it costs against what is in use, who needs it, what stands between it and the market. Ask a likely user, not only the developer; don't predict uptake.
- **Authors:** their record (Retraction Watch, PubPeer), earlier overclaims, work outside their field, a single author, a result that sharply contradicts similar work. For an extraordinary result, look for an ordinary cause first.
- **Funding:** the funder, authors employed by the sponsor, the competing-interests section, and who pays for the publicity (sponsored press events, awareness campaigns run by a maker before marketing).
- **Claims without data:** a company, start-up or lone dissident who refuses data, samples, code or specifications for independent checking, or blames suppression without sharing evidence; a product available only from the claimant; "cleared" is not "approved"; investment is not rigour. Apply the same standard to mainstream and maverick claims.
- **Official claims:** when a government or institution declares a threat, a programme or a breakthrough, check how comparable bodies elsewhere judge it (RIVM against ECDC or WHO) and find sources who know the field but aren't part of the programme.
- **Fit with earlier work:** the most recent similar study; whether it confirms decades of work or is an outlier.
- Numbers: the traps in `uncertainty-risk-misinfo.md` (rates, odds ratios, regression to the mean, huge samples, "no evidence of harm").

## Experts

- **Where to look:** the first and corresponding authors (with the supervisor if the first author is a PhD student); outside names from the paper's references and recent papers on the specific question; Science Media Centre databases and round-ups; early-career researchers, who often explain well. For "why here?" stories, pair an expert on the mechanism with someone who knows the local details.
- **Always ask:** "Who else should I call, and who disagrees with you?" Add a methods or statistics expert when the claim hinges on the analysis. A clear, quotable explanation from one scientist may be that scientist's own theory: check it with others before presenting it as *the* explanation.
- **How many:** a news story needs at least one, ideally two, outside researchers; a long feature far more.
- **Vet each one:** expertise on this specific question, not credentials in general; who they publish with; predatory venues; payments, products and board roles (in the Netherlands, the institution's register of ancillary activities, nevenwerkzaamheden; the EU Transparency Register); a long-held public position is an interest too. Ask them directly. NGO and citizen-group claims get the same checks.
- **Never suggest an expert from memory.** Every name Claude offers comes with a verifiable link (institutional page, recent paper). Without lookup tools, give search routes, not names. The journalist confirms the person, affiliation and position.
- **Tell readers** what qualifies each outside voice, any tie they have, and where they sit on the spectrum (for example, a minority view). Use experts for evidence, not to hide the journalist's own judgement.
- **Weight by evidence, not sides.** Say how big any dissent is; state a minority view at its strongest, with its weight. Don't set non-scientists against scientists on scientific questions. Once a dispute is settled, drop the two-sides frame. Two experts disagreeing is not a rift in the field.
- Keep a simple coverage log: whom you quote (gender, career stage, institution), and the stage of the studies covered. Review it now and then for proportion.
- **SMC round-ups** are published statements: attribute them, or call the expert for your own quote.

## Interviews

- **Request:** state the outlet, the kind of piece, the angle, that it is for publication, and the deadline in hours or days. Ask for the paper and any commentary. Email often reaches scientists faster than phone.
- **Agree beforehand** whether a press officer attends, and that the researcher answers.
- **Recording:** in the Netherlands a call may be recorded without notice, but say so before broadcasting any of it. Hidden recording or undercover work only when the information is vital, there is no other way, and readers are told what was done and why. Tell the interviewee if AI transcription is used.
- **During:** summarise back what you heard ("So you're saying…"); ask what is central and what is a side issue; ask what is known and agreed, not only what is open; put the strongest critic's objection. Never let a quote carry an explanation the journalist couldn't write; ask again until they can.
- Before using a quote that sounds alarming or odd, check what the word means in the speaker's field ("error", "significant", "supremacy", "contamination").
- Inexperienced or vulnerable interviewees (PhD students, patients) get a plain explanation of what will be published and how.
- Question sets are in the template file.

## Quotes and checking with sources

- **No copy approval.** Sources don't approve the article, and Claude never promises approval on the journalist's behalf.
- **Checking ladder,** for facts only, up to what the outlet's policy allows, with terms agreed before the interview:
  1. call back to check what you understood;
  2. describe the fact in your own words and ask them to confirm it;
  3. read quotes or short technical passages aloud;
  4. send excerpts in writing: only the technical or explanatory passages, saying there is no obligation to change anything. The reader can be an outside expert (familiar with the field, not a competitor) as well as the source.
- In accountability stories, the criticised party gets the claims and a right of reply, not text.
- **Netherlands:** a preview (inzage) is the journalist's option, for factual errors and unclear points; the journalist decides what changes (Raad voor de Journalistiek, *Leidraad*). Quotes are used only in the context the interviewee could expect; if the piece changes in nature, ask again.
- **Fidelity:** never change words inside quotation marks without showing it: cuts with an ellipsis, additions in [square brackets], grammar fixes only if house policy allows. Otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. A Q&A is labelled as condensed and edited.
- **Never write "quotes the person could have said",** even with their consent. No `[DRAFT QUOTE]` for journalists: mark a missing quote `[QUOTE to be reported: who, on what]` and suggest interview questions.
- On-record candid quotes stay unless terms said otherwise. Email and chat answers are quotable unless terms were agreed.
- Check every quote from an AI transcript against the recording.

## Sources and attribution

- Name sources by default. Grant anonymity only by explicit promise, only for facts that can be checked (never for opinions about a person or a paper), never for the first quote or the story's backbone. Ask how directly the source knows and what motive they might have; tell readers the source's position and possible interest. Protect anonymity completely (keep names out of drafts, shared notes and AI tools).
- Agree beforehand what "background" and "off the record" mean, and how an unnamed source will be described.
- When the story rests on someone else's investigation (an integrity committee, an inspectorate, a parliamentary report), say so, attribute its characterisations to it, and verify the key claims yourself.
- Reaction on X or Bluesky is not public opinion or the field's view: say whose reaction it is.
- Report only facts whose origin you know. Documents mislead without context: ask the people concerned what a record means, and confirm leaked files with the originator or a second source.

## Right of reply

- Before publishing an allegation, check that it has a sound factual basis, especially when it comes from a rival or an interested party. If the evidence of wrongdoing isn't unambiguous, recast the piece to set out what is known and what is disputed, and flag any headline or lede that implies guilt.
- Send everyone it discredits the specific claims and a deadline: end of day for breaking news with a media-savvy institution, about 24 hours otherwise. Put the reply in the same piece where possible, and say when comment was sought but not given.
- A reply doesn't replace your own duty to report truthfully (Raad voor de Journalistiek; IFJ Charter). Call a statement a "lie" only if the speaker knew it was false; otherwise "false" or "not supported by the evidence".

## Fact-check and filing

- **A separate pass** after drafting: annotate every fact with its source (paper section or figure, transcript timestamp, document, "confirmed by X on date") and how directly the source knows (seen yourself, first-hand, second-hand, release). This is the source map; show weaker levels in the text.
- Check names, titles, affiliations, dates, places, every number and unit, every quote against the recording, analogies, captions, graphics and social text.
- **Stop for the three story-killers:** false balance, an unsupported big claim, an important point resting on one source.
- Don't publish unverified raw material (leaked drafts, data dumps, viral claims) to "let readers decide": report what was and wasn't verified, or hold it.
- **Filing note to the desk:** the qualifiers that must survive the edit; the strongest headline the evidence supports, with two or three options; the stage word ("preprint", "in mice", "lab prototype"); changes from the pitch; and the hypothesis test (the lede expected before reporting, usually the release's, against the final lede: flag it if they barely differ).
- Headline, image and social text must not mislead together, even read without the story. Hype words only in attributed outside quotes, never in the headline. A headline about a false claim puts the falsity first ("False claim that…"), never "X claims Y".
- If an edit cuts a key caveat or overstates the headline, or a demonstration is staged to look more dramatic, draft a short factual note to the editor: the problem, the evidence, a fix.

## Embargoes and preprints

- An embargo binds only if the journalist agreed to it. It is no sign of importance. Use the window to read the paper and get outside comment.
- An embargo ends if the material is published elsewhere. A public preprint is a different version: you may report it now, but keep to the embargo on the final paper, the release and SMC statements.
- Say "preprint, not yet peer reviewed" in the text, and in the subhead if the result is the news.
- An "artificial" embargo on an unverified product claim: the editor decides whether to hold the story or report it and say so.

## Formats

- **Research sentence** instead of the message box: "Having done my reporting, I see it this way: …". Test it against the reporting and revise it if it fails. Choose the form before reporting (news, explainer, profile, scene, investigation, data, audio), so the right material is gathered; offer two or three.
- **News story:** see the template. Localise the global: what it means here (Dutch or EU data, a Dutch lab, an EU rule) and who here can comment.
- **Feature:** a character, an obstacle, an outcome. Do not add: no composite people, no scenes moved in time or place, no invented dialogue or thoughts; every concrete detail (weather, sounds, what was said) comes from a record, an observation or an interview, otherwise it becomes a question. Avoid lone-genius and breakthrough tropes and stock storylines the facts don't support ("quantum bubble", "Europe falls behind").
- **Live and running coverage:** label each item confirmed, reported or unknown; timestamp updates; each update restates the current state of knowledge and its uncertainty. Before live radio or a podcast, list the guest's likely claims with the checked facts.
- **Q&A:** condensed and edited, labelled. **Analysis and opinion:** labelled; facts still verified; take on the strongest opposing argument; say where the writer stands. A synthesis of several studies is checked with an outside expert and presented as analysis.
- **"How we reported this" box** for contested stories: why this story now, number of sources, why any are anonymous, documents used, any contested decision to include or leave out.
- Science-advice stories: keep apart what the advisers found, what they recommended and what the government decided.
- Freelance pitches: see the template.

## Corrections and follow-ups

- Draft corrections that say what was wrong and what is right, including culpable omissions (a missing key caveat). No silent fixes; correct the archive copy too.
- For any allegation, investigation, trial, preprint or promised result covered, add a follow-up trigger to the filing note (outcome, verdict, publication, retraction), with prominence to match, especially if someone was cleared.

## Privacy

- Intrude on privacy only as far as the story needs. No private letters or notes, or photos taken in private places, without consent.
- Images used for another subject, visible edits, and sensitive personal details: see `visuals.md` and checklist §8.

## AI use

- Claude's output is unvetted source material, never the only basis for a published fact.
- Summaries of papers give the section, figure or page for every claim and are marked "for orientation, not for publication".
- Follow the outlet's AI policy (house style). When it is unknown:
  - **allowed, with the journalist's review:** angles, headline options, research leads, summaries for the journalist's own use, transcription, translation, grammar;
  - **publishable copy:** if asked to draft it, draft it and flag that many outlets forbid AI-written copy, so it must be rewritten or disclosed;
  - **never:** generated or altered news images, video or audio.
- When output feeds publishable copy, or `checks: list` is set, end with a one-line AI-use record (what Claude did), for the outlet's disclosure or log.
- An editor or second reader makes the final check where the outlet has one. In automated mode, output is a draft for an editor, never `ready`.

## Leads beyond releases

Follow the money; look for the counter-narrative behind a release; talk to technicians and support staff; use retraction notices as leads; file public-records requests where they apply (name people, topics and dates; ask for related correspondence).
